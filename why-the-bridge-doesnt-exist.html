Thesis

The capital exists. The companies exist. The bridge doesn't.

Africa's growth-stage companies are profitable and ambitious. Europe's capital is actively looking for returns and impact. Both exist — so why do they so rarely meet? Because the thing between them was never built.

W
The Wajenzi teamCorporate-finance advisory
· 6 min read

Start with the thing almost everyone gets wrong. The problem with financing African growth isn't a shortage of good companies, and it isn't a shortage of money. Profitable, ambitious, growth-stage businesses exist right across the continent. European institutions, family offices and the diaspora are all actively looking for African exposure. The raw materials of a deal are sitting on both banks of the river. What's missing is the bridge.

That sounds almost too simple. But it explains a gap that trillions in global capital and decades of good intentions have failed to close.

A gap that isn't about money

The IFC puts the Sub-Saharan SME finance gap at roughly US$331 billion, across some 44 million formal businesses, more than half of them credit-constrained. It's tempting to read that as scarcity — not enough capital, not enough bankable companies. It isn't. It's structural.

These companies occupy a middle that the market has no rung for. They are too large for microfinance, which tops out long before a €5 million expansion. And they are too small, and too "frontier," for a traditional IPO or an international bank loan, which are built for household names raising hundreds of millions. They fall into the space between — a space local banks underprice and global markets can't reach. The gap isn't a judgement on these companies. It's the absence of a road.

Why neither side crosses alone

Left to themselves, the two sides never meet — and it's worth being precise about why.

The issuer's problem: the only routes to large-scale capital demand that a company become something it isn't yet. Convert to IFRS, restate years of accounts, carry a full prospectus, absorb the cost and calendar of a public listing. For most growth-stage firms, that burden kills the raise before it starts. They don't need capital that comes with a demand to stop being themselves.

The investor's problem: the appetite is real, but so is the hesitation. Buying a single, illiquid name in a jurisdiction whose legal protections you don't know is not an exposure most allocators will take. They need a recognised venue, familiar protections, real disclosure and a structure they can explain to their own risk committee. Without those, the capital stays home.

So each side waits for the other to move, and nothing does. The money and the merit are both present; the trust that would let them transact is not.

Capital doesn't lack appetite for Africa. It lacks a structure it can trust.

Trust is the real product

This is the insight the whole model turns on. A bridge isn't a pipe that money flows through — it's an engine that manufactures trust on both ends of the same transaction. You earn the issuer's trust by giving them a route that doesn't demand they become unrecognisable: home-country accounting, a proportionate listing, hands-on structuring. You earn the investor's trust by wrapping the opportunity in something they already recognise: an EU-domiciled vehicle, a listing on a venue like Luxembourg's Euro MTF, an ISIN, genuine reporting, often a development-finance anchor.

Do that, and a Rwandan agri-processor and a Belgian family office can finally be on the same page — the same instrument, the same protections, the same disclosure. Neither had to cross the whole river alone. The structure carried them.

Build the rails, not just a deal

If trust is the product, then the work is infrastructure, not opportunism. It's the patient assembly of rails: the venues, the vehicles, the licensed partners for every regulated step, the reporting that lets investors see what their capital is doing. That's also why the honest first move is advisory, not a fund or an exchange of one's own. The binding constraint in this market has never been a shortage of money or a missing trading screen — it's the shortage of investable issuers and trusted rails to carry them. You solve that one deal at a time, and each deal lays track for the next.

Early — on purpose

There's no pretending the track record is already there. But every credible market-maker started before its portfolio existed; what they had was a clear thesis, the right partners, and the discipline to do the regulated things properly. A young firm with that beats an old one with none. The capital exists. The companies exist. The bridge is the work — and building it, carefully and in the open, is the entire reason Wajenzi exists.
